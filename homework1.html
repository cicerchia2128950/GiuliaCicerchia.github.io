Public-Key Cryptography and Elliptic-Curve Scalar Multiplication

Elliptic-curve cryptography is based on the idea that a private key k can be transformed into a public key Q = kG, where G is a base point on the curve. The operation is easy to compute in the forward direction, but recovering k from Q is computationally difficult.

Curve: y² = x³ + 7 (mod 17)
Base point: G = (1, 5)
Private key: k = 6

1. Obiettivo

The goal of this exercise is to understand the basic mechanism behind elliptic-curve scalar multiplication in a toy setting. The idea is the same used in real systems such as Ethereum, even if here the curve and the key are much smaller.

3. Point addition

The key operation is point addition on the curve. For two points P and Q:

If P ≠ Q: λ = (y₂ - y₁) / (x₂ - x₁) mod 17
If P = Q: λ = (3x₁² + a) / (2y₁) mod 17

In this toy curve, a = 0. Then:

x₃ = λ² - x₁ - x₂ (mod 17)
y₃ = λ(x₁ - x₃) - y₁ (mod 17)

7. Statistical exploration

We repeat the experiment for several values of the private key k, and we inspect the distribution of the x and y coordinates of the resulting points. This gives us a simple statistical picture of how the outputs behave.

The outputs are not all equally frequent, but they do look spread over the curve. For a real cryptographic one-way function, we want outputs to appear unpredictable and to avoid any simple correlation with the input. In contrast, cryptographic hash functions are designed to produce a fixed-length digest with strong diffusion and avalanche properties. They are not the same as public-key generation, even though both are based on mathematical hardness.

8. Conclusion

The basic idea is very simple: a private key is a scalar, and the public key is the associated point on the curve obtained through repeated addition. This operation is used in real cryptographic systems such as Ethereum, which uses the secp256k1 curve. The toy example shows the intuition without using the full scale of real-world cryptography.
