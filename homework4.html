Homework 3 - Penetration process with n systems and m attackers

Random walk with i.i.d. increments X1, X2, ... as a model of the penetration score.

1. The model

There are n systems and m attackers. Each attacker goes through the systems one by one and Xi is the score obtained on the i-th system. Attackers are independent, so every attacker gives one trajectory S1, S2, ..., Sn of the same process. I use two models for Xi:

  • Bernoulli: Xi is 1 (system penetrated) with probability p and 0 otherwise. Mean μ = p, variance σ² = p(1-p).
  • Rademacher: Xi is +1 or -1 with probability 1/2. Mean μ = 0, variance σ² = 1. This is the symmetric case.

The three quantities studied are the total score Sn = X1 + ... + Xn, the average score Sn/n and the normalized score Sn/√n. From independence, E[Sn] = nμ and Var(Sn) = nσ², so Var(Sn/n) = σ²/n and Var(Sn/√n) = σ².

7. Comments

LLN. The average Sn/n of each attacker gets closer to μ as n grows. The fraction of attackers farther than ε from μ goes to 0 and stays under the Chebyshev bound, which is quite loose. So with many systems the average result of an attacker is almost deterministic.

Variance. Var(Sn) grows like n, Var(Sn/n) goes to 0 like 1/n, and Var(Sn/√n) stays equal to σ². The measured ratios in the plot are close to 1 for all three, with some noise since only m trajectories are used. Dividing by √n is the only choice that keeps the fluctuations visible without making them explode or vanish.

CLT. In the Rademacher case the histogram of Sn/√n gets closer to the standard normal and the Kolmogorov distance decreases with n. For small n the histogram looks like a comb because Sn only takes values with the same parity as n. With Bernoulli and p different from 1/2 the mean of Sn/√n is p√n, which grows, so the score has to be centered first (see the third trajectory plot, where it drifts away).

n and m. A larger n makes the total score larger (about nμ) and its spread larger (about σ√n), but the average becomes more precise. For the defender this means the percentage of penetrated systems can be predicted well when n is large, while the exact number cannot. The number of attackers m does not change the distribution of one attacker, it only changes how well we estimate it from the simulation: with larger m the Kolmogorov curve goes lower (section 6), roughly following 1/√m. If the scores of the m attackers are added together the result is a walk of length mn, so the average has variance σ²/(mn).

Limits of the model. I assumed independent systems with the same probability. In real systems vulnerabilities are shared, so the increments would be correlated and the variance of Sn would be larger than nσ².
