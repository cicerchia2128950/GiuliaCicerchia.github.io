Homework 4 - Welford's recurrence, sampling mean and variance

Exercise 1. Welford's recurrence formulas

Let x1, ..., xk be the data, Mk their mean and SSk = Σ (xi - Mk)² the sum of squares.

Mean (Knuth)

From k Mk = (k-1) Mk-1 + xk we get

Mk = Mk-1 + (xk - Mk-1)/k

Let d = Mk - Mk-1. Then two useful identities follow:

xk - Mk-1 = k d     and     xk - Mk = (xk - Mk-1) - d = (k-1) d

Sum of squares (Welford)

Since SSk = Σ xi² - k Mk², the difference between two steps is

SSk - SSk-1 = xk² - k Mk² + (k-1) Mk-1²

Using k Mk = (k-1) Mk-1 + xk, the term k Mk² = Mk((k-1) Mk-1 + xk). Substituting:

SSk - SSk-1 = xk(xk - Mk) - (k-1) Mk-1 (Mk - Mk-1)

By the identity above, (k-1)(Mk - Mk-1) = xk - Mk, so

SSk - SSk-1 = xk(xk - Mk) - Mk-1(xk - Mk) = (xk - Mk-1)(xk - Mk)

Final recurrences, starting from M0 = 0 and SS0 = 0:

Mk = Mk-1 + (xk - Mk-1)/k
SSk = SSk-1 + (xk - Mk-1)(xk - Mk)

The variance follows from SSk: the sample variance is s²k = SSk/(k-1) for k ≥ 2 (the version with k at the denominator is SSk/k). The product (xk - Mk-1)(xk - Mk) is never negative, so SS can only increase, and no subtraction of two large numbers is needed, which is the problem of the formula Σx² - kM².

Code

mean, SS = 0.0, 0.0
for k, x in enumerate(data, start=1):
    delta = x - mean          # x_k - M_{k-1}
    mean += delta / k         # M_k
    SS   += delta * (x - mean)  # (x_k - M_{k-1})(x_k - M_k)
variance = SS / (k - 1)

The three lines in the loop are the whole algorithm. The JavaScript version used in the simulation below is the same.

Exercise 2. Sampling mean and variance

Each penetration attempt gets a score in {0, 1, ..., 10} drawn from a fixed distribution with mean μ and variance σ². There are m hackers and each one attacks n systems, so each hacker has a trajectory of n scores. For every hacker I compute the sample mean and the sample variance (with n-1) using Welford's recurrences, then I average over the m hackers and compare with μ and σ². I also compute SS/n to see the difference with n-1.

Conclusions

Comparison with μ and σ². The average of the sample means is very close to μ for every n, and the average of the sample variances (with n-1) is close to σ². This is what we expect from unbiasedness: E[sample mean] = μ and E[s²] = σ². If SS is divided by n instead, the average is σ²(n-1)/n, which is visibly too small for small n (for n = 5 it is 80% of σ²) and the bias goes away when n grows.

Why it is useful in inferential statistics. In practice μ and σ² are not known and we have only one sample. Unbiasedness says that the sample mean and the sample variance do not systematically over or underestimate them, so we can use them as estimators. The other results say how reliable a single estimate is: the spread of the sample means is σ/√n, which is what confidence intervals and tests are based on, and it can be estimated by s/√n.

Effect of n. When each hacker attacks more systems, the sample means and the sample variances are less spread: the standard deviation of the sample mean decreases like 1/√n and the one of the sample variance also decreases about as 1/√n. The histograms become narrower around μ and σ², so a single hacker's sample is already a good estimate (consistency). The standard deviation of the variance is larger relative to its value than that of the mean, so more data is needed to estimate the variance well. Since the average over m hackers is already close to the target for small n, m only reduces the noise of the comparison, while n reduces the variability of each single estimate.
